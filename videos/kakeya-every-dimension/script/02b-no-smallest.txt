[storyteller, with a smirk, landing a punchline] Then Abram Besicovitch showed there is no smallest. You can turn the needle around in as little area as you like. A thousandth of a unit. A billionth. Pick any number above zero, and it fits.

[clear, explaining the trick on the picture, then dry] Here's the trick. Cut a fan of directions into thin slivers, then slide the slivers sideways so they pile on top of each other. Every direction is still there, but the slivers now share most of their room. Do that again and again, and the needle shuffles across them using almost no area. For turning, zero exactly is the one thing you can't do. A billionth is fine.

[clear, marking a contrast, defining a term the video will keep using] Now a different question. Forget the turning. Just lay down one copy of the needle in every direction, no motion needed. For that, the area can be exactly zero. Mathematicians call a shape like that a Kakeya set. Set just means a collection of points: any shape you could draw, however strange.
