[calm, painting a picture, with a question underneath] Now, waves. Picture a ring of ripples on a pond, all moving inward. How tall can they get when they meet? For an instant, they pile up into one tall spike. Then they pass through each other and spread out again. That's a wave coming to a focus.

[clear, explaining, with quiet confidence] To measure a spike, mathematicians raise the wave to a power, p, before adding it up. The higher the power, the more a tall, narrow spike counts. In 1991, Christopher Sogge asked how bad a focus can get, on average. At its worst instant, a wave can be enormously concentrated. But a focus can't last. Average over a stretch of time, and the wave is always tamer than its worst moment, by a definite amount. Sogge guessed exactly how much, up to an arbitrarily small loss. Averaging smooths the wave, locally in time, so his guess is called the local smoothing conjecture.
