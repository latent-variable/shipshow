[building, then showing on the picture] Now paint a Kakeya set. Look at two of its needles whose angles differ by a hair. The wide brush paints them as one stroke. Halve the brush, and they come apart into two. So every time you halve the brush, twice as many strokes count, each half as wide. Twice as many, half as wide: that alone is the same paint, like the filled square. If the strokes never overlapped, a Kakeya set would lose nothing. Only overlap can lose paint.

[clear, unhurried] However the needles sit, the paint can't be less than the tightest way to slide those strokes together, overlapping as much as they can. Take a first brush as wide as the whole piece: one stroke. Half the brush: two strokes, slid together. Then four, then eight, and on. That's the tree from before, round by round. Its paint: 100, 75, 52, 42, 35, 31, 27, 25.

[clear, reading the losses off the picture] Look at what each round loses. After a bumpy start: 20, 16, 13, 11, 10. Less and less. A shape like these, of 1.8 dimensions, needs about three and a half times the dabs each halving, so it loses about 13% every time, forever. The Kakeya losses drop below it.

[clear, landing the proof] And it's not just this drawing. In 1977, the mathematician Córdoba proved that every Kakeya set on a table, however it's drawn, loses its paint about this slowly, never much faster, give or take a fixed factor. So whatever dimension under two you guess, the Kakeya set ends up holding more paint than that shape. It has to be two dimensional.

[building, then letting a striking number land] Push it to the extreme: halve the brush 332 times, and it's about a googol times thinner. That's a one with a hundred zeros. A line's paint is down to a googolth. The Sierpiński triangle's, to under one part in a one with forty one zeros. A shape of 1.8 dimensions, to about one part in a one with twenty zeros. The most squeezed Kakeya set possible still needs about a hundredth.

[letting a surprising fact sink in, then clear] Its paint does run out, but only at no width at all, and absurdly slowly. Two dimensional doesn't mean losing nothing: it means losing less than any shape under two. By the paint test, a Kakeya set on a table is as two dimensional as a filled square. Zero area, full size.
