[building, then showing on the picture] Now paint a Kakeya set. Look at two of its needles whose angles differ by a hair. The wide brush paints them as one stroke. Halve the brush, and they come apart into two. So every time you halve the brush, twice as many strokes count, each half as wide.

[clear, unhurried] However the needles sit, the paint can't be less than the tightest way to slide those strokes together. The tree from before is one tight way. Its paint: 100, 75, 52, 42, 35, 31.

[clear, reading the losses off the picture] Look at what each round loses. After a bumpy start: 20, 16, 13, 11, 10. Less and less. A shape of 1.8 dimensions loses about 13% every time, forever, and the Kakeya losses drop below it.

[clear, landing the proof] And it's not just this drawing. In 1977, the mathematician Córdoba proved that every Kakeya set on a table keeps roughly this much paint, however it's drawn. So whatever dimension under two you guess, the Kakeya set ends up holding more paint than that shape. It has to be two dimensional.

[building, then letting a striking number land] Push it to the extreme: halve the brush 332 times, and it's about a googol times thinner. That's a one with a hundred zeros. A line's paint is down to a googolth. The Sierpiński triangle's, to under one part in a one with forty one zeros. The most squeezed Kakeya set possible still needs about a hundredth.

[letting a surprising fact sink in, then clear] Its paint does run out, but only at no width at all, and absurdly slowly. By the paint test, a Kakeya set on a table is two dimensional, like a filled square. Zero area, full size.
