[curious, posing a riddle to the viewer] So, a riddle. A Kakeya set can have no area at all. Is it basically nothing, a scribble of thin lines? Or is it, in some deeper sense, as big as the whole table?

[clear, explaining a simple test, unhurried] One way to measure size ignores area. Paint the shape with a brush, and keep making the brush thinner. Paint a line: halve the brush, and you need half the paint. 100%, then 50, then 25. The paint shrinks in step with the brush. That's what one dimensional means. Now paint a filled square. However thin the brush, you still have to cover the whole square, so the paint never goes down. That's two dimensional. A shape in between, a fractal, drops at a speed in between.

[building, then letting a surprise land] Now paint a Kakeya set. A thinner brush can tell apart directions that are closer together, so every time you halve the brush, there are twice as many strokes to paint, each half as wide. They're the slivers from before, and you slide them to overlap as much as you can. The line's paint halves every time. The Kakeya paint barely moves: 100, 75, 52, 42.

[clear, landing why it counts as full size] Here's why that matters. Every time the brush halves, a line loses half its paint, and any shape in between loses the same share every time. After the first few rounds, the Kakeya paint loses less and less. So in the end, it holds on to more than any shape in between.

[building, then letting a striking number land] Keep going, and make the brush a googol times thinner. That's a one with a hundred zeros. A plain line would be down to a googolth of the paint. A shape one and a half dimensional, to one part in a one with fifty zeros. The most squeezed Kakeya set still needs roughly a hundredth. So the billionth from before? That's for a needle with no width at all. With any width, you'd need a brush roughly a billion zeros thin.

[letting a surprising fact sink in, then clear] The paint barely goes down at all, slower than any shape in between, so by this test it's as two dimensional as a filled square. Zero area, full size.

[clear, opening up a bigger space] And math doesn't stop at a flat table. In space, a point needs three numbers to say where it is. In four dimensional space, it needs four, even though nobody can picture it. Call that number n. The Kakeya conjecture says that in n dimensional space, a set with a needle pointing every way is always full size: its paint barely goes down, like a solid block filling that space. Dimension n. The official version of the paint test is called Hausdorff dimension: same idea, stricter.
