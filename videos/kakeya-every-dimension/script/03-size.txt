[curious, posing a riddle to the viewer] So here's the riddle. A Kakeya set can have no area at all. Is it basically nothing, a scribble of thin lines? Or is it, in some deeper sense, as big as the whole table?

[clear, explaining a simple test, unhurried] Here's a way to measure size that doesn't care about area. Paint the shape with a brush, and keep making the brush thinner. For a single line, halve the brush, and you need half the paint: the paint shrinks in step. That's what one dimensional means. For a filled square, the paint never goes down, however thin the brush. That's two dimensional. Anything in between is in between.

[building, then letting a striking number land] Now a Kakeya set. Make the brush a googol times thinner. That's a one with a hundred zeros. A plain line would need a googolth of the paint. The Kakeya set still needs about a hundredth.

[letting a surprising fact sink in, then clear] The paint barely goes down at all, so by this test it's as two dimensional as a filled square. The Kakeya conjecture says the same in every dimension. A set with a needle pointing every way is always full size. Mathematicians call the official version of this test Hausdorff dimension: same idea, stricter.
