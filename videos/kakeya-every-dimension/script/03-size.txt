[curious, posing a riddle to the viewer] So here's the riddle. A Kakeya set can have no area at all. Is it basically nothing, a scribble of thin lines? Or is it, in some deeper sense, as big as the whole table?

[clear, explaining a simple test, unhurried] Here's a way to measure size that doesn't care about area. Paint the shape with a brush, and keep making the brush thinner. For a single line, halve the brush, and you need half the paint: the paint shrinks in step. That's what one dimensional means. For a filled square, the paint never goes down, however thin the brush. That's two dimensional. A shape in between, a fractal, drops at a speed in between.

[building, then letting a striking number land] Now a Kakeya set. Make the brush a googol times thinner. That's a one with a hundred zeros. A plain line would need a googolth of the paint. A shape one and a half dimensional would be down to a one with fifty zeros. The most squeezed Kakeya set still needs about a hundredth.

[letting a surprising fact sink in, then clear] The paint barely goes down at all, slower than any shape in between, so by this test it's as two dimensional as a filled square. Zero area, full size.

[clear, opening up a bigger space] And math doesn't stop at a flat table. In space, a point needs three numbers to say where it is. In four dimensional space, it needs four, even though nobody can picture it. Call that number n. The Kakeya conjecture says that in n dimensional space, a set with a needle pointing every way is always full size: dimension n. The official version of the paint test is called Hausdorff dimension: same idea, stricter.
